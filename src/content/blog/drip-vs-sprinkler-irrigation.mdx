Both drip and sprinkler systems are big improvements over flood irrigation, but they suit different crops, soils and budgets. Here's how I help farmers decide.

## How each system works

### Drip irrigation

Water is delivered slowly through emitters placed at or near the root zone. Only the soil around the plant is wetted, so evaporation and weed growth stay low.

### Sprinkler irrigation

Water is sprayed into the air and falls like rain over the whole area. Sprinklers suit closely spaced crops where wetting the entire field is useful.

## Comparing the options

| Factor | Drip | Sprinkler |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Water efficiency | Very high | High |
| Best for | Row crops, orchards, vegetables, polyhouses | Cereals, fodder, closely spaced crops |
| Wind sensitivity | None | Significant |
| Filtration needs | High (emitters clog) | Moderate |
| Fertigation | Excellent | Possible |

## Matching system to site

1. **Soil**: sandy soils favour drip with frequent short irrigations; heavy soils need low application rates to avoid runoff.
2. **Water source**: sediment-laden water means investing in sand and screen filters for drip.
3. **Topography**: pressure-compensating emitters keep drip uniform on slopes.
4. **Labour**: both reduce labour compared with flood irrigation, especially when combined with fertigation.

<Callout type="tip">
For vegetable growers in polyhouses, drip with fertigation is almost always the right starting point.
</Callout>

## Maintenance basics

- Flush laterals and clean filters regularly.
- Check emitter discharge at the start of each season.
- Inspect sprinkler nozzles for wear and replace them in sets for uniformity.

## Bottom line

Choose **drip** for high-value row crops and scarce water; choose **sprinklers** for closely spaced field crops on moderate soils. Either way, good design and maintenance matter more than the hardware itself.

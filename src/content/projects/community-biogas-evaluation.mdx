Community biogas plants promise clean cooking fuel and organic fertiliser from waste that is already on the farm. Yet many installations stop working within a few years. This thesis set out to understand **why some community plants thrive while others fail**, and which design and management factors make the difference.

## Objectives

- Evaluate the **technical performance** of community-scale digesters.
- Assess **social and institutional** arrangements: ownership, user committees and cost sharing.
- Identify the **economic** factors that influence long-term sustainability.
- Recommend practical improvements for design, operation and maintenance.

## Methodology

### Site selection and field survey

Plants were selected to cover different sizes, ages and management models. Each site visit combined a technical inspection with structured interviews.

### Technical assessment

The inspection checklist covered digester and dome condition, inlet/outlet structures, gas pipeline leaks, appliance condition, feeding regime, water-to-dung ratio and slurry management.

### Social and economic assessment

Household questionnaires and key-informant interviews explored who benefits, how costs are shared, how repairs are funded and how satisfied users are with the service.

## Analysis framework

Indicators were grouped into **technical, social, economic and environmental** dimensions and combined into a sustainability score for each plant, making it possible to compare sites and identify the weakest links.

## Key takeaways

1. **Operation and maintenance capacity** matters as much as initial construction quality.
2. Clear **ownership and user agreements** reduce conflict over gas distribution.
3. Planning for **slurry use** turns a waste stream into a visible benefit for farmers.

## Tools

Field surveys, structured questionnaires, spreadsheet-based scoring, and statistical analysis in Python.
